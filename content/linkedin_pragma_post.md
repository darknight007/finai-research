Long before "data science" was a job title, I was running price optimization algorithms inside Goldman Sachs.

A few years later, at a fintech, I was on the other side of that same coin — fraud models, credit underwriting engines, pricing and default-prediction systems, tuned from scratch on messy transaction data, long before anyone called it "ML."

Last year I went deeper — properly hands-on into the AI x Finance intersection, not just advising on it from the outside. And the further in I go, the more convinced I am of one thing:

The next real shift in this industry isn't happening in the chatbot layer.

Every bank and fintech I talk to right now is pouring its AI budget into voice models and workflow automation — copilots, contact-center agents, document summarizers. All useful. All visible. All the easy layer.

Underneath it sits something bigger, and far less touched: the actual data footprint these institutions have been sitting on for decades — transaction ledgers, customer profiles, risk tables, policy records. That's not a side asset. For financial services, that IS the business. And a new wave of "tabular foundation models" is emerging to treat that data exactly the way we've spent a decade treating language — pretrainable, transferable, reusable across tasks.

SAP just put a number on how real this is: over €1 billion committed to acquire Prior Labs, the team behind TabPFN, a foundation model built purely for structured, tabular data. That's not a research paper bet. That's boardroom capital.

And it isn't just SAP. Nubank, Adyen, Revolut and other large fintechs are already running versions of this bet internally — building proprietary foundation models over their own transaction data to power fraud, credit, and risk decisions at scale.

So I decided to stop reading about this shift and go build inside it — rebuilding Revolut's own tabular foundation model, PRAGMA, from its published architecture, and testing it, hands-on, against the models banks actually run in production today.

What I found changes how I'd advise any institution evaluating this category — where the real crossover point sits, and what has to be true before this bet pays off.
